Navigation and a multi-room home

A robot vacuum finds its way in a way the sheet should name. The three that show up are bump or random, a camera, and lidar-style mapping. A sheet that only says smart has not told you which. A review of a different navigation system, even under the same family name, describes that other robot. A robot that drives itself is a different product from a vacuum you push. This page stays with the robot.

Bump and random

A bump robot drives until it hits something, then turns. A random pattern covers floor by crossing it again, not by remembering rooms. It can clean one open room. In a home with several rooms, doorways, and furniture, it can stay in the room where it started, miss a room, or spend the battery recleaning the same patch. It does not build a map you can edit. You cannot send it to one room, and you cannot draw a no-go zone, because it has no floor plan to edit.

A physical barrier, or a magnetic strip if the sheet lists one, is a different way to keep it out of a spot. Some sheets say the robot uses a gyro. A gyro tracks direction. It can make the path less random. It is still not a room map unless the sheet says the robot saves rooms. If the sheet does not say which, you do not yet know.

Camera

A camera robot uses a camera to see the space and, when the sheet says so, to build a map. Read where the sheet says the camera looks, and whether the robot needs the lights on. A camera that needs light can lose its place in a dark room. A camera aimed at the ceiling and a camera aimed forward are different claims. One is about finding the robot's place. The other is about seeing what is in front of it. A sheet that only says camera has not said which.

A camera can also be a privacy question. Read whether images stay on the robot or are sent with an account, and what the robot does if you decline. If the page is silent, that is an unknown. Do not take the answer from another model in the family.

Lidar-style mapping

Lidar-style navigation uses a distance sensor, often a spinning one, that measures how far surfaces are. When the sheet says so, it builds a floor plan. It supplies its own signal, so it does not need the room lights the way a camera can. Glass, mirrors, and thin chair legs can still confuse a distance sensor. The sheet will not promise a perfect map. It will tell you the sensor this model uses.

The useful part for a multi-room home is the map, not the word on the badge. A map you can open lets you see rooms, send the robot to one of them, and mark a place it should skip. A lidar-style robot whose sheet does not mention a saved map is a different product from one that does. A bump robot and a mapping robot can share a family name. Match the sensor on this sheet before you compare a price.

A map and obstacle avoidance are different claims. A robot can know the rooms and still run over a cable or a pet mess if the sheet does not list a way to see objects in front of it. Read that line. If it is missing, you do not yet know.

A home with one open room can be a fair job for a bump robot. A home with hallways, more than one floor covering, and rooms you want cleaned on different days is a job for a map. The percent off does not turn one into the other. A map of one floor is not a map of the next. If you would carry the robot upstairs, read whether the sheet says it keeps more than one map. A doorway threshold the robot cannot climb can keep even a mapped robot out of a room. The sheet may list a threshold height. If it does not, that height is an unknown. If the home has stairs or a ledge, read whether the sheet says the robot detects a drop. A sensor on another model does not transfer.

Suction claims and what it picks up

The number next to suction is a lab claim. Pascals (Pa) are a unit of pressure. Air watts are a different unit. A higher figure is a higher claim on that unit. It is not, by itself, a cleaner floor. Two sheets that use different units are not in the same comparison. Do not convert one into the other unless this sheet does. A badge that says the suction is powerful, and gives no number and no unit, is a phrase.

What reaches the bin depends on more than that figure. The brush has to reach the dirt. The seal between the robot and the floor changes on hard floor, a low rug, and a taller carpet. Edges and corners depend on a side brush or an edge mode, if the sheet lists one. Hair, grit, and larger crumbs are different pickups. A sheet that only lists a pascal figure has not told you about edges, carpet, or hair. If the sheet lists a carpet boost, that line is about this model, and it is a claim, not a test on your rug. A missing line is an unknown. Do not copy the figure from a review of a similar name.

Compare the number only with another robot whose sheet uses the same unit, then still read the brush and the floors you have. The robot that picks up what is on your floor is the one to price. The larger badge is not that test.

The dock and the parts you keep buying

A self-emptying dock draws the dirt out of the robot's bin and into a bag or a container in the base, so you empty the robot less often. The robot still has a small bin of its own. The dock is a second piece. It needs floor space, an outlet, and any clearance the sheet names. A robot sold without that dock is a different product from the same family name with the dock in the box. Read the cart. A photo that shows a dock does not mean the dock is included.

Then read what you keep buying. Bags, if this dock uses them. Filters. The main roller and the side brush. A mop pad, if this model mops. The sheet may say a part is washable and reused, or that it is replaced. A bagless dock and a dock that only takes a bag for this model are different ongoing costs. Spare parts are listed for a model number. A bag or a roller listed for the widely sold model is not automatically a part for the number in the cart.

The sheet may list a suggested interval for replacing a bag, a filter, or a brush. If it does, that interval is a claim about this model. If it does not, the interval is an unknown. Do not copy one from another model. Add the price of the parts you will actually keep buying, for as long as you plan to keep the robot, to the checkout price before you call the percent off the whole cost. If the listing does not show those part prices, that cost is still unknown. A low robot price next to a bag you can only buy for this model is a different offer from a low price on a robot whose parts you have already priced.

If the sheet names a filter standard, that line is about this model. A badge that says the filter helps with allergies, and names no standard, is a phrase. If the sale bundles the dock, extra bags, or extra filters with the robot, price the robot on its own and price only the extras you would buy anyway. How to take that package apart is in Black Friday bundles: real savings or padding?

Mop pads and carpet

A mop combo is a robot that vacuums and also wets a pad. The pad is the part that decides what happens on carpet. Read which motion this sheet describes. A drag pad is a cloth the robot pulls across the floor. It wipes. It does not scrub. A lifting pad raises so it does not touch the carpet. A spinning pad turns while it wipes. A pad that vibrates is another kind. A badge that only says mop has not told you which is in the cart.

A drag pad stays on the floor, including a rug, unless you take it off or the sheet says it lifts. A spinning pad or a vibrating pad can still wet a carpet if it does not lift. Read whether the sheet says the lift happens when the robot detects carpet, or only when you mark that carpet yourself. If the sheet is silent, you do not yet know. A no-go zone over the rug is a different fix, and it needs the map in the next section.

A robot that vacuums and mops in one pass and a robot that mops only after you attach a plate are different products. Read whether the vacuum still runs while the pad is down, and whether you can vacuum a carpet without the pad attached. Water is a second system. An onboard tank is water you fill. A dock that washes the pads and refills the tank is a different product, with its own floor space, and sometimes a cleaning solution the sheet names. That solution, if you have to keep buying it, belongs with the bags and the filters. A pad you rinse in the sink is a different chore from a dock that washes the pad. Neither one answers the carpet line. The percent off does not either.

The app, no-go zones, and an account

No-go zones, room names, a schedule, and a map live in an app when the sheet says they do. A no-go zone is a line that keeps the robot out of a spot: a bowl, a cable, a rug you do not want mopped. That line needs a map. A bump robot has no map to draw on. Its barrier, if the sheet lists one, is physical. A mapping robot whose zones exist only in the app does not have those zones when you decline the app.

Read whether this model cleans from the button on the robot with no account, or whether the first clean, the map, and the schedule require an account and a network. Those are different products. A feature that exists only in the app is not a feature of the button. If you will not create an account, the features that require one are features you will not have. Read which phones the sheet says the app supports. If the page does not say what still works with no account, that is an unknown. Do not assume the core clean works offline.

Read what the listing says happens to a saved map if you skip the account or later close it. A map of your rooms is a map of your home. If the sheet says images or the map are stored with the account, read how you delete them, including before a return. If the page is silent, you do not yet know.

Battery runtime and the home you have

Runtime on the sheet is a claim under a condition the sheet may or may not name. A quiet mode on hard floor and the strongest suction on carpet can be different runtimes. If the sheet lists one number, read which mode and which floor it belongs to. A number with no mode is hard to put next to another model's number. A battery capacity is not a runtime. Read the minutes.

Minutes are not the size of your home. If the sheet also lists an area, that area is another claim. Read whether the sheet says the floor and the mode behind it. If it does not, you do not yet know what the area measures. Doors, clutter, and carpet take longer. A small apartment and a multi-room home are different jobs. Match the minutes to the floor you will actually clean. Do not treat a coverage badge as a measurement of your rooms.

Read whether the robot returns to a charger, recharges, and resumes when the battery runs down before the job is done. A self-emptying dock and a charger-only base are different. Resume uses the base that is actually in the cart. If resume is missing from the sheet, a battery that dies mid-clean may leave rooms undone. A missing line is an unknown. A runtime you remember from the reviewed model does not transfer.

Pet hair and the roller

Pet hair winds onto a brush. A tangle-free claim means the sheet says this roller resists that wrap. Read what the roller is. A rubber roller and a bristle brush are different parts. The word on the badge is a claim until the sheet names the roller. If the sheet does not describe it, the claim is a phrase.

Hair still has to go somewhere: the bin, the filter, the dock bag, and often the side brush. A tangle-free main roller does not, by itself, include the side brush. You may still clear hair from the ends and replace the filter. Read whether a replacement roller and a replacement side brush are sold for this model number. A roller listed for another model in the family is not automatically yours. Tangle-free does not mean no maintenance. It means the sheet is claiming less hair wrapped on the roller it names. The return window is how you learn whether that claim holds for the hair in your home. The window, a restocking fee, and a final-sale label are in Black Friday return policies.

Noise

The robot makes sound while it cleans, and a self-emptying dock can make a shorter, louder sound when it empties the bin. Those are different noises. The sheet sometimes lists a decibel figure and sometimes lists none. If it lists one, read the distance and the mode, including whether the figure is the robot driving or the dock emptying. A number without a distance is hard to put next to another number. If the sheet lists no figure, you do not have a measurement. A badge that says quiet has not filled that in.

Two models in one family can sound different because the motor, the suction mode, and the dock differ. A review of the other model does not answer for this one. A schedule, if the sheet says the app has one, is how you choose the time of day. The return window is how you learn the sound in your home, including who pays to ship a robot and a dock back. That check is in Black Friday return policies.

Sale-only models and last year's robot

The family name is a group. The model in the cart is the robot. A sale can offer a near-identical variant, sometimes only then. One change can mark a camera instead of lidar-style mapping, a robot with no dock, a mop that does not lift, a shorter runtime, or a different bag. The photo can match the robot people reviewed. The line in the cart is the robot you would receive.

Last year's model is a different robot too. It has had time to show what it usually sells for. This year's model may have a short chart, or a list price it has not been selling at. A large percent off this year's list price can look bigger while checkout sits above what last year's robot actually sells for. Put the two sheets side by side. Read the navigation, the dock, the mop, and the warranty.

When last year's robot still has the map and the dock you need, and its checkout is at or under that model's own recent prices, it can be the better buy. That checkout price belongs to last year's robot. Judge each against its own prices. A chart for the current model does not answer for the older one, and the older one's low does not answer for a sale-only variant.

Write the full name from the cart, including any number. Match it to the spec sheet and to the price chart before you compare a price. If the cart shows a shorter name than the sheet, stop and find the full one. If you cannot find a sheet for the exact model, pause. You are being asked to buy a robot you cannot compare.

The same kind of mismatch is why a Black Friday TV model number may not match the set in a review, and why a kitchen appliance can be a seller-exclusive model under a familiar family name. On a robot vacuum the differences sit in the navigation, the dock, the mop, and the battery. The warranty length on this model's sheet is the coverage you are buying. A length you remember from the reviewed model does not transfer.

The badge math can be tidy and still describe the ordinary price. The dollar price at checkout is the fact. This model's own recent selling prices are the other fact. About 30 to 90 days, when the chart has them. Thirty days shows a short markup just before the sale. Ninety days is the safer window when that model has already been on sale more than once. Returns and the warranty

Running the robot to see whether it maps your rooms can count as opened. Read whether this offer treats a tested robot as opened, charges a restocking fee, or marks it final sale. A robot and a dock are bulky. Who pays return shipping belongs in the price you compare. The general rules for a holiday window, a restocking fee, an opened item, and a final-sale label are in Black Friday return policies. The policy linked from this cart is the one that applies. If the page does not state a window, find the policy before you pay. A missing line is an unknown.

If the app built a map of your home, or stored images, read how you delete them before you send the robot back. If the listing is silent, you do not yet know.

The warranty on this model is printed for this model. The robot, the battery, and the dock can be separate lines, with different lengths. A length you remember from the reviewed model does not transfer. If the listing is silent, you do not yet know what you already have. A protection plan offered at checkout is a second purchase. It does not add the dock the cart left out, and it does not turn a sale-only model into the one a review covered. How to price that plan is in extended warranties and protection plans.

A refurbished or open-box robot can be the same model at a lower checkout price, with a worn roller, an original battery, a used filter, or a different warranty. When to wait

Wait when the sheet cannot answer the checks yet. A missing navigation type, a dock you cannot tell is in the cart, or a model you cannot find a sheet for is a reason to stop. The percent off can stay on the page while you look those up. It is not evidence that the price is low.

Wait when you have not priced the bags, filters, and brushes you will keep buying. Wait when a mop will meet carpet and the sheet does not say the pad lifts, or when the no-go zones and the schedule exist only in an account you will not create. A feature you will not have is not part of the discount.

Wait when today's checkout sits near the typical price, or only under a reference price this model has not been selling at. A holiday label does not make that a new low. The named Friday and the named Monday are not automatically the cheaper day for this robot. Compare the two against this model's own prices, which is the point of Black Friday vs Cyber Monday. An early label, weeks before the weekend, is the same comparison, covered in early Black Friday deals.

Wait, or at least keep the claim in its place, when the cart is a sale-only variant or last year's robot and the chart you are reading belongs to the other one. You can buy it when the sheet matches what you need and you accept the price next to a similar model's recent low. The badge is not what makes that decision.

You do not have to wait when the model in the cart matches the sheet and the chart, the navigation fits the rooms you have, you have priced the dock and the parts you will keep buying, and checkout is at or under this model's own recent low. A later label that only ties that price is the same price with a different headline. If the robot has to arrive by a date, the delivery date can matter more than another dip. A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the spec sheet, this model's prices, and the return terms on this offer.

  1. 1Does this robot bump, use a camera, or use lidar-style mapping, and is that enough for the rooms I have?
  2. 2Is the suction figure a unit I can compare, and does the sheet say anything about carpet, edges, and hair?
  3. 3Is the self-emptying dock in the cart, and what will bags, filters, and brushes cost for this model?
  4. 4Does the mop pad drag, lift, spin, or vibrate, and what does it do on carpet?
  5. 5Do no-go zones and a schedule work from the button, or only with an account I am willing to create?
  6. 6Do the runtime minutes match this home, and does the robot resume after it recharges?
  7. 7Is the model in the cart the one I mean, or a sale-only variant or last year's robot?
  8. 8Is today's checkout at or under this model's own recent low, after the parts I will keep buying, and what do the return window and the warranty say?

When the model matches, the navigation fits the rooms you have, and checkout is at or under this model's own recent low after the parts you will keep buying, the deal is about this robot. If the chart belongs to another name, or the dock in the photo is not in the cart, skip the percent off until you have this model's price.

Questions people ask

Does a higher suction number mean the robot cleans better?

A pascal figure or an air-watt figure is a lab claim, and the two units are not the same comparison. A higher number is a higher claim. It does not, by itself, say what the robot picks up on your floor. Read the brush, the floors you have, and whether the sheet says anything about edges and carpet. A missing line is an unknown.

Which navigation matters in a home with more than one room?

A bump or random robot covers floor by running into things and turning. It does not build a map you can edit. A camera, or a lidar-style distance sensor, can build a map when the sheet says so. The map is what lets you send the robot to one room and set a no-go zone. Match the sensor on this sheet. A badge that only says smart has not named it.

What do I keep paying for after a self-emptying dock?

The dock needs bags or a bin the sheet names, plus filters, rollers, and a side brush for this model. A mop can add pads and a cleaning solution. Add the parts you will keep buying to the checkout price. If the listing does not price those parts, that cost is an unknown. A bag or a roller for a different model in the family is not automatically the one this robot uses.

Will the mop wet the carpet?

A drag pad stays on the floor unless you remove it or the sheet says it lifts. A lifting pad raises. A spinning pad is a different motion, and it can still wet a carpet if it does not lift. Read whether the lift happens when the robot detects carpet or only where you mark a zone. If the sheet does not say, you do not yet know.

Do no-go zones work without an account?

A no-go zone is a line on a map. If the map and the zones live in an app account, declining the account declines those zones. Read whether the button on the robot starts a clean with no account. A feature that exists only in the app is not a feature of the button. If the page does not say what works with no account, that is an unknown.

Is last year's robot, or a sale-only model, the one in the review?

Last year's robot is a different robot. A sale-only variant can share the family name and drop the dock, the mapping sensor, or the mop lift. A price, a review, or a chart for the other one answers for that other robot. Match the model in the cart to the sheet and the chart before you compare.
