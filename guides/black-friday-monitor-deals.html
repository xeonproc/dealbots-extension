Black Friday monitor deals: what to check before you buy

Updated November 7, 2026

A monitor deal can look like a deep cut and still be a softer picture, a refresh rate the panel does not run, or a near-identical model that exists for the sale. Screen size alone does not say how sharp it is. The spec sheet, the full model number, and that model's own prices tell you whether the discount is on the monitor you meant to buy.

Resolution and screen size

Resolution is the pixel count, two numbers on the sheet. Screen size is the diagonal. Pixel density is how those two sit together. The same pixel count on a larger screen spreads the pixels over more glass, so the picture looks softer up close. A smaller screen with that same count looks sharper, because the pixels are packed tighter.

A headline that only names the size, or only names the resolution, has not told you the density. Read both. Two monitors can share a size and list different resolutions. Two can share a resolution and list different sizes. The pair on this sheet is the picture you are buying. A sheet that also prints a density figure is giving you the same comparison in one number. If that figure is missing, the two lines above it are enough to compare.

Where you sit changes whether a softer panel bothers you. A monitor on a desk is close. A television is usually across a room, which is why a size and a resolution that look fine on a set can look soft at arm's length. How to compare a television's size and native resolution is in Black Friday TV model numbers. The built-in screen on a laptop is its own line, covered in Black Friday laptop deals. This page is the separate monitor.

Read which resolution is the panel's own count. A figure the monitor can accept from a computer, often written as "up to" or "supported," is the signal it will take. It is a different number from the pixels in the glass. If the sheet lists one resolution and does not say it is the panel's, treat the panel count as an unknown until you find the line that names it.

Refresh rate and response time

The refresh rate is how many times a second the panel can draw a new picture. The sheet prints it in hertz. A higher number can look smoother in games and in fast motion. Office work and video do not depend on the highest number on the page. Read the rate you will actually use, at the resolution you will actually run.

A headline hertz figure is a claim until the sheet ties it to the panel and to a port. The panel's native rate is the one that describes the glass. A higher figure can be a mode you have to turn on, and it can apply only when the resolution is lowered. One input can carry the headline rate while another cannot. If the sheet does not say which port carries which rate, at which resolution, you do not yet know what you will see.

Response time

Response time is how quickly a pixel changes, printed in milliseconds. A lower number is the faster claim. Sheets do not all use the same test. Gray-to-gray measures a shift between two shades. A method that tracks a moving image can print a different number on the same panel. If the sheet gives a millisecond figure and does not name the test, you cannot line it up with a figure from another sheet. The number is a label until the test is named.

Variable refresh rate, when the sheet lists it, lets the monitor follow the rate the computer is sending instead of holding one number. It is a separate line from the maximum rate. A missing line is an unknown. A similar model's spec sheet does not fill it in.

Panel types

The panel type is the way the screen makes the picture. The four names that show up on monitor sheets are IPS, VA, OLED, and TN. A sheet that does not name one has not told you which you are buying. A review of a different type, even at the same size, describes that other monitor.

IPS

IPS holds its color and its brightness better when you are not sitting straight in front of it. That matters if you share the screen, or if you move in your chair. Dark scenes can look slightly lifted compared with a VA or an OLED panel. The sheet will not always describe that. The type is the clue, and it is the line to compare when two monitors share a size.

VA

VA usually shows darker blacks than a typical IPS panel. That is the contrast people are pointing at. The picture can shift more when you view it from the side. Fast motion can look smeared compared with an IPS or a TN panel. If you sit centered and you care about dark scenes, read the type before you treat two same-size monitors as the same product.

OLED

OLED makes light in each pixel, so a dark area can go dark without a backlight glowing behind it. A bright logo, a taskbar, or a window that stays in one place for a long time can leave a faint mark. That is image retention. The sheet may mention a feature that shifts the picture slightly to reduce it. If you will leave the same windows up all day, that line is part of the decision. An OLED television is a different product. The set version of the panel check is in Black Friday TV model numbers.

TN

TN is the panel that often shows up at the lower price, next to a fast response-time claim. It can change pixels quickly. Color and brightness shift more when you move your head, and the picture is the one most likely to look washed out from the side. A low price on a TN panel is a low price on that kind of picture.

None of these names is a score by itself. Match the type to how you sit and what you look at, then compare prices among monitors of that type. A cheaper IPS and a cheaper TN are different monitors. The percent off does not close that gap.

HDR labels

HDR on a monitor is a claim that the picture can show a wider range from dark to bright. On a doorbuster, the letters are often a badge. A badge that only says HDR has not told you how bright the panel gets, or whether it can dim one part of the picture while another part stays bright.

Read the brightness line if the sheet has one. Sheets that list it use nits. A missing brightness line means you do not know. Local dimming, when the sheet lists it, is what darkens part of the picture instead of the whole panel at once. A monitor can say HDR and leave that line off. Without it, bright and dark move together, and a dark scene does not stay dark just because the box says HDR. A numbered tier on the box is still a label until brightness and dimming are on the sheet.

The formats matter when you know the computer or the game will send one. A sheet that names the formats is more specific than a sheet that only says HDR. If you will not use those sources, the badge is not a reason to pay more, and it is not evidence that the discount is real. The same word on a television, where the formats are part of comparing two sets, is in Black Friday TV model numbers. Leave that set's HDR line on the set. It does not describe this monitor.

Ports and the cable in the box

The port is how the picture gets to the monitor. Count the inputs, then read what each one can carry. HDMI and DisplayPort are the two that show up most on a computer monitor. They are different plugs. A cable for one does not fit the other. USB-C is the third, and it is the one that can also charge a laptop, when the sheet says it does.

Read the version or the capability listed for each port. An older HDMI port can cap the resolution, the refresh rate, or both, below the number in the headline. DisplayPort can be the input that carries the higher rate, or HDMI can be, depending on this sheet. If the sheet does not say which port carries the headline resolution and rate together, do not assume every input does.

USB-C and power delivery

USB-C needs two facts, not one badge. Video means the port can carry the picture. Power delivery, with a wattage, means the port can charge a laptop. A USB-C port that only lists data does neither. Compare the charging wattage on the monitor's sheet with the wattage your laptop's own sheet says it needs. A monitor wattage below that will not keep the battery charged. The ports on the laptop itself are a separate check, covered in Black Friday laptop deals.

The cable in the box is part of what you are buying. The sheet should say whether an HDMI cable, a DisplayPort cable, or a USB-C cable is included. A cable that cannot carry the headline rate leaves you short of the spec on the badge, even when the port could have done it. A USB-C cable that only carries charging will not carry the picture. If the sheet does not name the included cable, that is an unknown. If you will have to buy the cable that actually works, add it to the checkout price before you call the discount real.

The stand and a VESA mount

The stand is what you adjust every day if the monitor is not on an arm. Read whether it does height, tilt, swivel, and pivot into a portrait orientation. A stand that only tilts is a different product from one that raises to eye level. Leaving the height adjustment off is one way a sale model costs less. If you need the screen at eye level, a stand that does not raise means a stack of books or an arm you buy separately.

VESA is the hole pattern on the back that an arm or a wall mount bolts to. The sheet should name that pattern, two numbers for the spacing of the holes. The mount has to match those numbers. Some monitors have the holes in the open. Some hide them behind a plate. Some sale models leave the holes off, or include them only with a bracket that is not in the box. If the sheet does not mention a pattern, an arm is an unknown. Do not take the pattern from a different size in the same family.

A monitor you will mount still needs the pattern, and the weight if the sheet lists one. The stand can come off on one model and be fixed on another with a similar name. Read that line for the number in the cart. The feet on a television, and whether a soundbar fits under them, are a different stand, covered with the rest of the set in Black Friday TV model numbers.

Sale-only model numbers

The series name is a family. The full model number is the monitor. A sale can offer a near-identical variant that exists for the season, sometimes only then. One changed character can mark a different panel, a lower refresh rate, a lower resolution, a stand that does not raise, fewer ports, a USB-C port without power delivery, or a shorter warranty. The photo can match the monitor people reviewed. The number in the cart is the monitor you would receive.

Write the full number from the cart, including any suffix. Match it to the spec sheet and to the price chart before you compare a price. A low for the year-round number, or for the same size with a different panel, is a low for that other monitor. A review of a different number describes that other monitor too. If the cart shows a shorter number than the sheet, stop and find the full one. If you cannot find a sheet for the exact number, pause. You are being asked to buy a monitor you cannot compare.

The same kind of mismatch is why a Black Friday TV model number may not match the set in a review, and why a laptop under a familiar series name can be a thinner configuration. On a monitor the differences sit in the density, the panel, the ports, and the stand. The warranty length on this model's sheet is the coverage you are buying. A length you remember from the reviewed model does not transfer. A protection plan offered at checkout is a second purchase, covered in extended warranties and protection plans.

A sale-only model can have little price history of its own. The percent off can point at a list price this exact version has rarely sold at, or has not sold at. The monitor can still be a fair buy. It is a different product from the reviewed one until the sheets say otherwise.

When to wait

Wait when the sheet cannot answer the checks yet. A missing resolution, a missing panel type, a refresh rate with no port attached to it, or a model number you cannot find a sheet for is a reason to stop. The percent off can stay on the page while you look those up. It is not evidence that the price is low.

Wait when today's checkout sits near the typical price, or only under a reference price this model has not been selling at. A holiday label does not make that a new low. The named Friday and the named Monday are not automatically the cheaper day for this monitor. Compare the two against this model's own prices, which is the point of Black Friday vs Cyber Monday. An early label, weeks before the weekend, is the same comparison, covered in early Black Friday deals.

Wait, or at least keep the claim in its place, when the cart is a sale-only variant with a short chart or none. You can buy it when the sheet matches what you need and you accept the price next to a similar model's recent low. The badge is not what makes that decision. A chart you do not have yet is a reason the percent off is still a label.

You do not have to wait when the full model number matches the sheet and the chart, the density, panel, ports, and stand are the ones you will use, and checkout is at or under this model's own recent low. A later label that only ties that price is the same price with a different headline. If the monitor has to arrive by a date, the order-by date can matter more than another dip. That check is in holiday shipping cutoffs. The months when electronics commonly go on sale are in the best time to buy. The calendar is a reason to look. The chart for this model is the reason to pay.

A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the spec sheet, and this model's prices.

  1. 1Do the resolution and the screen size, together, give me the sharpness I want at the distance I sit?
  2. 2Which refresh rate does this panel run, on which port, and which test is behind the response-time number?
  3. 3Is the panel IPS, VA, OLED, or TN, and does that match how I will look at it?
  4. 4Does the sheet list brightness or local dimming, or does it only say HDR?
  5. 5Do the HDMI, DisplayPort, or USB-C ports, including power delivery and the cable in the box, cover the computer I will connect?
  6. 6Does the stand adjust the way I need, and does a VESA pattern match the mount I have or plan to buy?
  7. 7Is the full model number the monitor I mean, or a near-identical variant sold for the sale?
  8. 8Is today's checkout price at or under this model's own recent low, and is there a reason to wait?

When the model number matches, the density, panel, ports, and stand are the ones you will use, and checkout is at or under this model's own recent low, the deal is about this monitor. If the chart belongs to another size or another number, skip the percent off until you have this model's price.

Questions people ask

Does a bigger monitor with the same resolution look as sharp?

A larger screen with the same pixel count spreads those pixels over more glass, so the picture looks softer up close. Read the resolution and the size together. A resolution the monitor can accept from a computer is not the pixel count of the panel. At a desk you sit closer than you would to a television across a room, so a size that looks fine on a TV can look soft on a monitor.

Is the refresh rate in the headline the rate the panel runs?

The headline rate can be higher than the panel's native rate, it can apply only at a lower resolution, and one port can carry it while another cannot. Response time is printed in milliseconds, and the number depends on the test. Gray-to-gray and a method that tracks a moving image can print different numbers for the same panel. A millisecond figure that does not name the test is a label.

What do IPS, VA, OLED, and TN mean for a monitor buyer?

IPS holds its color better when you are not sitting straight in front of the screen. VA usually shows darker blacks, a narrower viewing angle, and more smear in fast motion. OLED lights each pixel on its own, so dark areas can go dark, and a static image left in one place can leave a mark. TN is often the lower-priced panel, and the picture shifts more when you move your head. The type named on this sheet is the one you are buying. A missing type is an unknown.

Does an HDR badge mean the monitor is brighter?

A badge that only says HDR does not tell you the brightness, or whether the panel can dim one area while another stays bright. Read the brightness line if the sheet has one, and look for local dimming. A numbered HDR tier is still a label until those lines are on the sheet. Without local dimming, the whole picture gets brighter together.

What should I check on ports, cables, and the stand?

Read whether HDMI, DisplayPort, or USB-C carries the resolution and refresh rate together, and whether a USB-C port lists video plus a charging wattage. The cable in the box has to be able to carry that signal. On the stand, read height, tilt, swivel, and pivot. A VESA pattern is the hole spacing for an arm or a wall mount. If the sheet does not name the pattern, an arm is an unknown.

How do I tell if a monitor discount is real, and when should I wait?

Read the price history for this exact model number, not the percent off on the page and not the chart for a similar name or a different size. The percent uses a reference price the seller chose. Wait when the sheet is missing lines you need, when checkout only beats a reference price this model has not been selling at, or when a sale-only model has no chart of its own. A checkout price at or under this model's own recent low, on the specs you will use, is the one that is actually lower.
