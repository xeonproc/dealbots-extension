Cyber Monday software and subscription deals: what to check before you buy

Updated November 27, 2026

A security suite, a VPN, a password manager, cloud storage, office or creative software, a streaming or music plan, or a learning app can show a low price for the first term and a higher price when it renews. The renewal, the people who will use it, and the last day you can cancel tell you what the offer costs.

In 2026, Black Friday is Friday, November 27, and Cyber Monday is Monday, November 30. Next year the Friday moves. The checks below do not. The intro price and the renewal

The price due today is one term. The renewal is the price the terms say you will be charged when that term ends. A large percent off the first term does not describe the later terms. That gap is the first-year trap: the headline is the cheap term, and the years you keep are the renewal, unless you cancel before it.

Write the price due today. Write the renewal. Count the terms you would actually keep. Add the renewal once for every term after the first. That sum is the cost of the stretch you would keep. Divide by the number of terms for a per-term figure, or by the number of months for a per-month figure, and compare two plans only over the same stretch. If you would cancel before the renewal, the total is the first term, and only if canceling on the printed terms stops the next charge. If the renewal is not printed, do not guess it. Any stretch longer than the first term is unknown.

A "was," "list," or "compare at" figure is a reference the seller chose. On a plan it may be the renewal, a longer term, or a price this plan has not been charging. A digital plan may have no public chart of past checkout prices. When a chart exists, read it for this plan and this term, as in how to read a price history chart. When it does not, the comparison you can finish is the first term against the renewal on this offer. How a reference price turns into a percent is in how to tell if a sale is real.

A suite that puts a security tool, a VPN, and a password manager in one checkout is a package. Count a part only if you would pay for it alone. That split is in Black Friday bundles: real savings or padding? A learning app can sell one course, which ends, or a plan that bills again. Read which checkout this is.

Office or creative software can open your files only while the plan is paid. Read what still opens if you stop: the files, an export, or nothing. A password manager or a cloud plan is useful after you cancel only if you can take a copy out. Read whether the terms allow an export. If they do not say, check before the account closes.

Auto-renew, a reminder, and turning it off

Read whether renewal is already selected. A selected box is the default on this offer. A page that never mentions renewal has not told you the default. The next charge uses the date and the amount the terms name, which may be the renewal rather than today's price.

Put a reminder on a calendar you control, on a day before the last day the terms let you cancel and still avoid that charge. If the terms do not say how many days of notice they need, set the reminder early enough that you can still find the account. An email from the seller is a reminder only if the terms promise one, and only if it arrives before the deadline. Do not treat a message you have not been promised as the plan.

Turning renewal off is the control the terms describe, usually in the account's billing or subscription settings. Deleting the app, closing the window, or stopping use does not cancel a plan the terms say will renew. Save the confirmation when the account shows one. If it shows none, the setting you can still see on the account page is the record. If you cannot find a control, and the terms do not say how to cancel, ask before the charge date and treat renewal as still on.

Several years paid now, or monthly

A prepaid block is the years or months you pay now. A monthly plan is one month at a time. Compare the prepaid checkout with the monthly price times the same number of months, for the months you would keep. A smaller per-month line on a prepaid offer is the cheaper one only across that whole block, and only if you will use it.

If you might stop partway, the prepaid block can cost more than the monthly payments for the months you used, unless the terms refund the unused time. Read whether they do. Also read whether the block ends or renews into another block. "Two years" can mean two years and then another charge, or two years and then it stops. Those are different offers. A monthly plan you can leave is not the same product as a prepaid term you cannot get back.

What a "lifetime" deal is promising

"Lifetime" is a word on the offer until the terms define it. It can mean the life of the product, the life of your account, the life of the company, or a long fixed term with that word on the headline. Read the definition on this offer. If the terms never define it, you do not know what you bought.

If the product is discontinued, or the company stops operating, a lifetime purchase can end with it. The terms may say the license ends, may name a successor, or may say nothing. If they say nothing, you do not know what you keep. A lifetime price can also leave out a later account fee, a cloud add-on, or a newer version. Add any of those you would keep. If a price for one of them is missing, the total is unknown.

Compare the lifetime checkout with the renewal times the years you expect to use it. If the lifetime checkout is higher, it costs more over that stretch. If you cannot name the years, or the renewal is missing, you cannot finish the comparison. If lifetime is tied to the life of the product or the company, that comparison is a ceiling. It is not a promise that those years will exist.

Devices, seats, and a family plan

Read how many devices, how many people, and whether those devices can be used at the same time. A license for one computer does not cover a phone unless the terms say so. A streaming or music plan can list a number of profiles and a smaller number of streams at once. Those are two limits. A learning seat, a cloud allotment, or a security install can each have its own cap. Read the one you will hit.

A family plan's price per person is the plan price divided by the people who will actually use it, not by the maximum printed on the page. An empty seat does not lower the bill. A one-person plan can cost less than a family plan once you divide by the people you have. A seat on a work or school account that you do not control can end when that membership ends. That is a different plan from one you pay for yourself.

A promo on a plan you already pay for

Read whether the offer says new customers, a first term, or not for an account that already subscribes. An offer limited that way is not a discount on the plan you already pay for, unless the terms say it applies to an existing account. The price you can get is the one those terms allow: the renewal, a different tier, or no reduction. Opening another account so the offer treats you as a new customer is not a path to count on. Price the path your current account is allowed to take.

A code stacked on a sale price counts only after the cart accepts it. Read whether that code changes the renewal or only the first term. A code the cart applies today, and the terms never mention at renewal, changes the first term only. Switching from monthly to a longer term, or from a lower tier to a higher one, can be a different offer from a new signup. Read whether this promo applies to a change on an account you already have.

Region, device, and allowed use

The terms list countries, the store you download from, or the computer and phone systems the plan runs on. A plan that does not list your country or your device may not run, or may not include the catalog in the ad. A streaming or music plan can limit where playback works. A VPN or a security tool can limit which uses the terms allow. Read those lines. A use that is missing from the allowed list is not included. If the page is silent, the limit is unknown.

A download locked to one kind of computer does not follow you to another unless the terms say it will. An intro price tied to a region can change at renewal, or if the account's region changes. Read that line if you might not stay where the offer applies. If the page does not say, the later price is unknown.

Refunds and cancellation on a digital plan

A digital plan can have a shorter refund window than a boxed item, or no refund once a code is redeemed or the software is installed. Read this offer. Canceling stops a future charge when the terms say so. It refunds the current period only when the terms say so. Read both. Write down the last day you can cancel and still avoid the next charge.

The seller of a code and the company that runs the plan can name different windows. Read the one you paid. Holiday return windows, restocking fees, and what opened means for other goods are in Black Friday return policies. That holiday window may not be the window on this plan. The page in front of you is the one that applies.

A gift card spent on a subscription

A gift card, store credit, or spend-and-get bonus helps on this purchase only for the part you will spend on a plan you already wanted. A card that excludes digital goods, subscriptions, or renewals is worth nothing here. Read the exclusion list. A bonus that arrives after you subscribe does not lower today's checkout until you can spend it, and it does not change the renewal unless the terms say the credit applies to a later charge.

How to turn a face amount into the part you will use, and how that compares with a straight discount, is in Black Friday gift card bonuses and 'spend $X, get $Y' promos.

A free trial that becomes a charge

A trial can be no charge today and the renewal price on the day the trial ends. Read both amounts, and the date of the first charge, before you enter a payment method. The trial converts to paid unless you cancel on the schedule the terms print. The cancel path is the one in the account. It may not be the button that started the trial. Deleting the app does not, by itself, end a trial the terms say will convert.

A trial of something you already receive another way becomes a second bill if you let it convert and you did not need the extra. The same reminder you would set for a renewal belongs on a trial: a day you choose, before the charge, on a calendar you control.

A plan you may already have

Before you pay, look at what a mobile phone plan, a home internet plan, a card or bank benefit, and a school or work account already include. This page does not name a carrier, a provider, or a card. The terms on your own account are the list. If an included version covers the use you wanted, the sale price is a second bill for the same job.

The included version can cover fewer devices, a shorter term, a smaller catalog, or fewer features. A perk that ends when you close the card or change the phone plan is not the same length as a plan you pay yourself. Read how long the included one lasts. If that length is not written down, you do not know. A password manager on a work account may not cover personal devices. Read the device line before you treat the two as the same, and before you pay for both.

A three-year comparison, using made-up numbers

This example is hypothetical. Every amount below is made up. These figures are not a real listing, a typical price, or a prediction. Replace each one with the number on your page.

Plan A, in this sketch, is an intro year at $36 and a renewal of $90 a year. Kept for three years, that is 36 + 90 × (3 - 1) = 36 + 180 = $216. Per year, 216 ÷ 3 = $72. If you cancel before the renewal, and the terms stop the next charge, the total is $36.

The badge can print a percent by comparing 36 with 90: (90 - 36) ÷ 90 = 54 ÷ 90 = 0.6, which is 60 percent. That percent is the first year of this sketch. Three years at the $90 renewal would be 90 × 3 = $270. The sketch's three-year total is $54 less, because 270 - 216 = 54, and 54 ÷ 270 = 0.2, which is 20 percent across the three years. Both percents are arithmetic on these made-up figures. They are not a discount to expect, and they are not a typical gap between an intro price and a renewal.

Plan B, in this sketch, is $150 paid once for three years. The terms in the sketch say it does not renew and does not refund unused time. The three-year total is $150, or 150 ÷ 3 = $50 a year if you keep all three. If you stop after the first year, you still paid $150. Plan A's first year was $36.

Plan C, in this sketch, is $9 a month. Thirty-six months is 9 × 36 = $324. Four months is 9 × 4 = $36. Four months of Plan C matches Plan A's first-year cash, without the $90 renewal. Thirty-six months of Plan C costs more than Plan A or Plan B in this sketch. The cheaper line is the one that matches how long you will keep it.

A family plan in this sketch is $120 a year and allows up to six people. Six people who will use it is 120 ÷ 6 = $20 a person. Two people is 120 ÷ 2 = $60 a person. One person pays $120, which is more than Plan A's $90 renewal in this sketch. Empty seats do not lower the $120. If any of these prices were missing on a real offer, that offer's total would be unknown.

When to wait

Wait if the renewal, the term length, the seat count, the region, or the cancel path is missing. Wait if the offer is limited to new customers and you already subscribe, and the page does not show the price your account can get. Wait if "lifetime" is undefined, or if you have not checked a phone plan, a home internet plan, a card benefit, or a school or work account that might already include this. The calendar sits in Black Friday vs Cyber Monday and early Black Friday deals. Buy when the total for the stretch you would keep, for the people who will use it, is the number you meant to pay, and the cancel date is written down.

A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the terms, and the accounts you already have.

  1. 1What is the price this term, and what is the renewal after it?
  2. 2For the terms I would keep, what is the total, and what is that per year or per month?
  3. 3Is renewal already on, and what day will I check the account before the next charge?
  4. 4Is a prepaid block cheaper than monthly only for the months I will keep, and does the block renew?
  5. 5What does "lifetime" mean here, and what happens if the product or the company stops?
  6. 6How many devices or people, and what is the price per person who will actually use it?
  7. 7Does this promo apply to an account that already has the plan, and does a code change the renewal?
  8. 8Does it work in my country and on my devices, and what is the refund window once it is installed?
  9. 9Do I already get this from a mobile plan, a home internet plan, a card benefit, or a school or work account?

Questions people ask

How do I compare an intro price with the renewal price?

The intro price is this term. The renewal is the next charge. Add the renewal once for every later term you would keep. Divide by those terms if you want a per-term figure. If the renewal is missing, any stretch longer than the first term is unknown. The dollar figures elsewhere on this page are made up.

How do I stop a subscription from renewing?

Read whether renewal is already selected. Put a reminder on a day before the last day the terms let you cancel. Turn renewal off in the account the terms name, and keep the confirmation. Deleting the app does not cancel a plan the terms say will renew.

When is a lifetime software deal cheaper than paying each year?

When the lifetime checkout is lower than the renewal times the years you expect to use it, and the terms' definition of lifetime still covers you for those years. If the product or the company stops, the purchase can end. If the definition or the renewal is missing, you cannot finish the comparison.

How do I price a family plan or a multi-device plan?

Divide the plan price by the people who will actually use it, not by the maximum the page allows. Empty seats do not lower the bill. Compare that per-person figure with a one-person plan for the same term. A device cap and a simultaneous-use cap are different limits.

Can I use a new-customer promo on a plan I already pay for?

Only when the terms say the offer applies to an existing account. If the line says new customers, a first term, or not for current subscribers, your price is the one those terms allow, which may be the renewal. A code counts after the cart accepts it, and it changes the renewal only when the terms say so.

What if a phone plan, an internet plan, or a card already includes this?

Read what you already have before you pay for a second copy. An included version can cover fewer devices, a shorter term, or fewer features. If it covers the use you wanted, the sale price is another bill. The terms on your own account are the ones that apply.
