Black Friday headphone and earbud deals: what to check before you buy

Updated November 3, 2026

A headphone or earbud deal can look like a deep cut and still be last year's model, a weaker feature list, or the same pair at a price it has already sold for. The generation, the specs that change how they sound and fit, and that model's own prices tell you whether the discount is on the pair you actually want.

Last year's model, sold as new

Headphones and earbuds move in generations. A current model and last year's model can share a family name and look almost the same in the product photo. The older one can still be sealed, unused, and listed right next to the newer one. "New" on that listing describes the condition. It does not mean this year's model. A discount on a new pair of last year's model is a sale on that older model.

The older model is often how a seller clears stock once the newer one exists. That can be a fair buy. It is a miss when the family name made you think you were getting the current one. Write the full name from the cart, including any generation, suffix, color, or region. Match it to the spec sheet before you compare a price. One different character can mark a different pair. The headline is a label. The name in the cart is the pair you would receive.

What changes between those two sheets is often the part you live with: the kind of noise cancelling, the codecs, the battery claim, the fit, the water-resistance rating, and whether the pair can stay connected to two devices at once. Sometimes the newer model is a small change. Sometimes the older one gives up a line the current sheet lists. The sheets say which. The family name does not.

A sale on last year's model can be the better buy when those specs still do what you need and checkout is low for that exact model. Last year's model has had time to show what it usually sells for. This year's model may have only a short price history, or a list price it has not actually sold at. A big percent off this year's list price can look larger while checkout is not lower than last year's model. Put the two sheets side by side, then judge each price against that model's own recent prices.

The form factor is part of the match. Earbuds, on-ear cups, and over-ear cups can share a family name and be different products. So can a version that includes a charging case, a cable, or a wireless transmitter and a version that leaves one of those out. If the listing is a pair plus a speaker, a second set, or another extra priced as one package, price the pieces separately. That comparison is in Black Friday bundles: real savings or padding?

The specs that change the pair

Open the sheet for the model in the cart. If you cannot find one, pause. You are being asked to buy a pair you cannot compare. Read these lines. A missing line is an unknown. It is not a match for another model in the same family.

Noise cancelling type

Two different things get called noise cancelling. Passive isolation is the seal. The ear tip or the ear cup blocks sound before it reaches you, and it depends on how well that seal fits your ear. Active noise cancelling uses microphones and processing to reduce a steady low sound, such as a cabin rumble or a fan. Voices and sudden sounds are a weaker match for that processing. A pair with active cancelling and a pair that only seals are different products, even when the family name matches.

Read which one the sheet lists. "Noise cancelling" alone has not told you. Some sheets also list a mode that lets outside sound in on purpose, sometimes called transparency or ambient. That is a separate line from cancelling. Note it if you need to hear an announcement, traffic, or a person in the room. If the sheet does not say whether cancelling is active, passive, or both, you do not yet know what you are buying.

Codec support

A codec is the way audio is compressed on the way from your phone, tablet, or computer to the pair. The sheet names the codecs this model supports. The device you listen from has to support that codec too, or the pair falls back to a simpler one both sides share. A listing that advertises a higher-quality codec is only that codec when your device speaks it.

Match the codec list on this sheet to the device you will actually use. Two models in one family can support different codecs. A review of the other model does not answer for the pair in the cart. If the sheet does not name the codecs, that line is an unknown, and a badge that says "high quality" has not filled it in.

Battery life claims

The sheet lists a claim about how long a charge lasts. On earbuds it often lists a second claim for the case that holds extra charges. On over-ear headphones there may be no case figure, only the hours on one charge. Those numbers are the result of the test they chose: a set volume, a set codec, noise cancelling on or off. Your day can be shorter. Active cancelling, a higher volume, and a codec that draws more power all use the battery faster.

Read whether the hours include cancelling. A claim that only holds with cancelling off is a different number from the one you will get if you leave it on. If the sheet does not say, the figure is less specific than it looks. Also read the charge time if it is listed. A case with a dead battery is a pair you cannot recharge away from a plug. The earbud claim and the case claim are two numbers. Compare both when you put last year's sheet next to this year's.

Fit and ear tips

Fit is the part no spec sheet can settle for your ears. Earbuds may ship with tips in more than one size, or with only one. Over-ear and on-ear cups use a pad and a shape the sheet rarely describes well. A tip that does not seal changes the bass, the passive isolation, and whether active cancelling has a seal to work with. Read which tip sizes or pads are in the box, and whether you can swap them.

If you already know a tip shape that will not stay in your ear, a listing that includes only that shape is a worse match, even at a lower price. The photo will not tell you. The "in the box" list will, when it is specific. The only way to know the fit is to wear them, which is why the return window later on this page matters as much as the sheet.

Water-resistance ratings

A water-resistance rating is a code on the sheet, usually starting with IP. The first character is dust, or an X when dust was not part of the test. The second character is water. A higher water character is a tougher lab test. A 4 is a splash test. A 5 is water jets. A 7 is immersion at a depth of one meter for 30 minutes. The test has a set time and a set way of applying water. It is not a promise about sweat every day, a shower, a pool, or a rinse under a tap.

Some pairs list a code. Some list none. Some list a code for the earbuds and a lower one, or none, for the case. The case is the part you should not rinse. "Sweat resistant" or "water resistant," with no code, is a phrase. Two models in one family can carry different codes. The code printed for the model in the cart is the one you are buying. Compare the characters. Leave the percent-off badge out of that comparison.

Multipoint

Multipoint means the pair can stay connected to two devices at once, so a call on a phone and audio on a computer can both be live without pairing again. A sheet that says multipoint, or that the pair connects to two devices at once, is claiming that. A pair that only remembers several devices, and connects to one of them at a time, is a different feature. The memory is convenient. It is not the same as two live connections.

Read the line on this model's sheet. Another model in the family can have multipoint when this one does not. If the line is missing, do not assume the pair can hold two connections because the newer model can, or because a review of a similar name said so.

Why the list price is often inflated

Headphones and earbuds are a category where the list price and the price people pay often sit far apart. The number next to "was," "list," or "compare at" is a reference the seller chose. It may be the price from when the model launched. It may be a figure this exact model has rarely sold at. The selling price can settle lower while the list price stays on the page. The percent off gets large. Checkout can still be the price the pair has already been selling for.

That gap gets wider once a newer generation exists. Last year's model can keep its original list price in the headline while the everyday price dropped months ago. A sealed, new listing of that older model then shows a steep percent off a number it no longer sells at. The same thing happens to a current model whose launch list price was never the price shoppers paid a month later. The badge math can be tidy and still describe the ordinary price.

None of that tells you today's checkout is high or low. It tells you the percent is not the evidence. The dollar price at checkout is the fact. How a reference price inflates the percent off, including a short markup just before a sale, is in how to tell if a sale is real.

Check this model's price history

A price chart answers for the model on the chart. A low for this year's model, for a different form in the same family, or for last year's similar name, is a low for that other pair. Search the exact model in the cart. Match the condition. New, open-box, and refurbished are different offers, and they do not share a chart. How those offers compare during the sale is in refurbished vs new on Black Friday.

Read about 30 to 90 days when the chart has them. Thirty days shows a short markup just before the sale. Ninety days is the safer window when that model has already been on sale more than once. The typical price is where offers cluster. The recent low is the bottom. Those shapes are in how to read a price history chart.

A current model can have a short chart because it has not been on sale for long. On a short chart, the first advertised price and the sale price can both sit inside the same brief window. Read the dates. If the "was" price only appears for a few days before the sale, or never appears as a selling price, the percent off is a label. Last year's model usually has the longer chart. Use that chart for last year's model only. You can set today's checkout for the new model next to the older model's recent low and decide whether the spec differences are worth the gap. That does not turn the older model's low into this model's low.

The return window is a fit test

The spec sheet can be right and the pair can still hurt, fall out, or fail to seal. Fit is personal, and the return window is how you find that out without keeping a pair you cannot wear. Read the window on this listing before you buy. Count the days. See who pays return shipping. See whether a pair is returnable once the seal is open or the tips have been tried.

Some listings accept a fit return. Some treat opened audio as final sale, charge a restocking fee, or require the hygiene seal to stay intact. A seal that must stay intact means you cannot try the tips and still send them back. The fit test and the return cannot both happen. A seasonal listing can also carry a different window from the ordinary one for the same model. A usual window may shrink for the holiday or stretch later into winter. The policy on this page is the one that applies. The general version of that check, including holiday windows, gifts, and restocking fees, is in Black Friday return policies.

Write the date you would have to ship them back, and whether you will be home to wear them inside that window. A low price on a pair you cannot return after one listening session is a different offer from the same price with a fit window. If the listing does not state a window, find the policy before you pay. A missing line is an unknown. A later drop in the price is a different question from a pair that does not fit. A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the spec sheet, this model's prices, and the return policy.

  1. 1Is this the generation and full model I mean, or last year's model under a similar name?
  2. 2What kind of noise cancelling does the sheet list, and which codecs does this pair support on the device I will use?
  3. 3Do the battery claims say whether noise cancelling is on, and do the ear tips, pads, and water-resistance code fit how I will wear them?
  4. 4If I need two devices connected at once, does multipoint appear on this model's sheet?
  5. 5Is today's checkout price at or under this model's own recent low, not just under a list price?
  6. 6Can I try the fit and still return them, who pays shipping, and by what date?

When the specs match what you need, checkout is at or under this model's own recent low, and the return terms leave you a way to test the fit, the deal is about this pair. If the name is last year's model, or the chart belongs to another generation, skip the percent off until you have this model's price.

Questions people ask

Is a sale on last year's headphones a good deal?

It can be, when the specs still do what you need and the checkout price is low for that exact model. New describes the condition, not the generation. Last year's model is a different product from this year's model with a similar name. Compare the two spec sheets, then compare today's price with what that older model has actually been selling for.

What is the difference between passive isolation and active noise cancelling?

Passive isolation is the seal. The ear tip or the ear cup blocks sound before it reaches you, and it depends on fit. Active noise cancelling uses microphones and processing to reduce a steady low sound, such as a cabin or a fan. A sheet that only says noise cancelling has not told you which one you are buying. If the line is missing, you do not yet know.

Which headphone and earbud specs should I check?

Read the noise cancelling type, the codecs the pair supports and whether your phone, tablet, or computer supports them too, the battery claim and whether it includes cancelling, the ear tips or pads in the box, the water-resistance code, and whether multipoint is listed for this model. A missing line is an unknown. It is not a match for another model in the same family.

Why do headphone and earbud list prices look so high?

The list price is a reference the seller chose. In this category it often stays at the figure from launch while the selling price has already moved down, especially after a newer generation arrives. A large percent off that old list price can describe a pair that has been selling for today's checkout price already. The dollar price is the fact. The percent is arithmetic on the reference.

How do I tell if a headphone discount is real?

Read the price history for this exact model, not the percent off on the page and not the chart for a newer generation with a similar name. The percent uses a reference price the seller chose. A checkout price at or under this model's own recent low is the one that is actually lower.
