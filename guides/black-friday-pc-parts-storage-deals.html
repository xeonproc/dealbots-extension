Black Friday PC parts and storage deals: what to check before you buy

Updated November 16, 2026

A graphics card, a drive, or a memory kit can look like a deep cut and still be a different board, a slower kind of flash, or a speed the motherboard will not run. The chip or the capacity on the box, the interface, the power supply, and this part's own prices tell you whether the discount is on the part you meant to buy.

The same chip on a different graphics card

The chip is the part that draws the picture. The board around it is the card you receive. The same chip ships on cards with a different cooler, a different length, a different thickness, different power plugs, and a different amount or type of memory. Write the full name from the cart, not the chip name in the headline. Match that name to the sheet.

Read the memory amount and the memory type. Less memory, or a slower memory type, under the same chip name is a different card, and it has its own price. A shorter cooler, a blower-style cooler, or a cooler that fills more expansion slots is a different card too. The boost clock on the sheet is this board's clock. A small step up from another board with the same chip is not a different chip, and it does not borrow the other board's recent low.

Last-generation cards on clearance

A previous-generation card on clearance is a sale on that card. It has had time to show what it usually sells for. A newer card may have a short chart, or a list price it has not been selling at. A large percent off the newer list price can look bigger while checkout sits above what the older card has actually been selling for.

The older card is the better buy when it does the work you have, it fits, the supply can run it, and its checkout is at or under that card's own recent prices. It is a different product when you needed the newer chip. Judge each card against its own prices. A laptop that uses a chip of a similar family is a different computer, covered in Black Friday laptop deals. This page stays with the card you install in a desktop.

Power and whether the card fits the case

The sheet lists a recommended power-supply wattage and the power connectors the card needs. Compare the wattage with the number printed on the supply you already have. The recommendation is the card maker's figure for a whole computer, not a measurement of yours. If your supply is below what the sheet asks for, the card comes with a second purchase. The checks for a supply you are buying are in Power supplies.

Count the plugs. A card can need one or more of the older PCIe power plugs, or a 16-pin plug. An adapter in the box covers a plug your supply does not have. An adapter sold separately is another purchase. Use the adapter that came with this card. A cable from a different supply can fit a socket and still be wired for that other supply. Use the cables that came with the supply you have.

Then measure. The sheet lists the card's length and how many slots the cooler occupies. The case sheet lists the longest card that fits, and a drive cage or a front radiator can shorten that. A card that does not fit is not a deal. If either sheet is missing the measurement, pause.

SSD interface and form factor

An internal drive has an interface and a shape, and both have to match a free spot in the computer. SATA and NVMe are different interfaces. NVMe uses PCIe. A sheet that only says M.2 has not said which interface it is. A SATA stick and an NVMe stick can share a shape, and neither runs in a slot that accepts only the other.

PCIe 3.0, PCIe 4.0, and PCIe 5.0 are generations. The drive runs at the slower of the drive and the slot. A PCIe 5.0 drive in a PCIe 3.0 slot runs at the slot's generation for as long as it sits there. Paying for the faster generation pays off when the slot and the work can use it. Each generation, at each capacity, has its own price. Match the line on the cart to the chart.

The common stick size is M.2 2280: 22 mm wide and 80 mm long. The sheet may also say 2230, 2242, or 22110. A longer stick does not fit a shorter mount. A 2.5-inch SATA drive needs a bay, a SATA data cable, and a power plug. A board with no free SATA port, or a case with no free bay, does not take that drive. Some M.2 slots share lanes with a SATA port or with another slot. The motherboard sheet says which ports turn off when that slot is filled. A drive that disables the port your other drive uses is a different plan. A heatsink on the stick can also be too tall for the cover the board already has. Read the height.

DRAM-less drives, and QLC versus TLC

Some drives keep a map of where the data lives in a DRAM chip on the drive. A DRAM-less drive keeps that map in a slice of the flash, or in a little of the computer's memory. The sheet says which. A DRAM-less drive can be a sound buy for files you mostly read. A large copy, or other sustained writing, is the case to read about before you choose it. If the sheet does not say, you do not know.

TLC stores three bits in each cell. QLC stores four. The sheet names the flash. More bits in a cell is a reason the endurance line can be lower on a QLC drive than on a TLC drive of the same capacity. The letters are not a speed, and they are not the price. Read this listing. A QLC drive can be the right buy for photos or video you mostly read. A drive you will install programs on, or write to every day, is the case for reading the endurance line before you take the lower checkout. A speed on the box can be a short burst into a faster cache, not the speed of the rest of the drive once that cache is full. "Up to" is a ceiling.

Labeled capacity, usable space, and price per terabyte

Drive makers label capacity in decimal units. One terabyte on the box is 1,000,000,000,000 bytes. The system often reports those same bytes with a binary unit. The binary unit people still call a terabyte is 1,099,511,627,776 bytes, so the number on the screen is smaller. Formatting uses a little more, and a system you install uses more still. The box is not promising the binary figure, and it is not promising the free space left after the system is on the drive. The gap is the counting method. It is not storage the seller kept.

Price per terabyte is the checkout price divided by the labeled capacity on that box. Use one unit for every drive. On the box's scale, 1 TB is 1,000 GB, so a 500 GB drive is half a terabyte. Do not divide one drive's price by its gigabytes and another drive's price by its terabytes and then compare the two results. A lower price per terabyte is a lower price for the capacity printed on the box. It is not a lower price for a drive that will not fit, a flash type you did not want, or a capacity you will not fill. If you need two terabytes, a four-terabyte drive costs less only when its checkout is lower than the two-terabyte drive you would actually buy. Divide when you are choosing among drives you would fill. Compare checkout totals when you are choosing a size.

Warranty and the endurance rating

When the sheet lists an endurance rating, it is printed as terabytes written, shortened to TBW. That figure is how much data the warranty expects this drive to write. A higher TBW is more rated writes. Compare it across drives of the same labeled capacity. A larger drive's rating belongs to that larger drive. Set it next to a smaller drive's number only after you have said which capacity each figure is for.

Read whether the warranty ends at a number of years, at the endurance rating, or at whichever the sheet says comes first. A shorter warranty is a smaller offer, even when the price is lower. The terms on this listing are the ones that count. A length you remember from another drive does not transfer. A protection plan in the cart is a separate purchase, priced in extended warranties and protection plans.

External drives

An external drive is a drive in an enclosure, or a spinning drive in one. The plug is the interface. USB 3.2 Gen 1, Gen 2, and Gen 2x2 are different rates, and a box can say USB 3.2 without saying which. USB 4 is another step. The sheet has to name the rate. The port on the computer and the cable have to support the rate you are paying for. The slower end wins. A cable in the box that is rated below the drive caps the drive.

The enclosure can hold an NVMe stick or a SATA stick. The words "external SSD" do not say which, and they do not say you can open the box and use the stick in a desktop slot. A portable hard drive is a spinning drive. It is a different product from an external SSD, with its own price. Read whether the drive draws power from the port or needs a power brick. The capacity on the box uses the same decimal count as an internal drive. The chart for an internal stick of the same capacity is not this drive's chart.

Memory kits

DDR4 and DDR5 are different generations, and the notch is in a different place. A DDR5 kit does not fit a DDR4 slot. A desktop board does not take the shorter module built for a laptop. The motherboard sheet names the generation. Match the kit to that line. Memory soldered inside a laptop is a different product, covered in Black Friday laptop deals.

The kit lists a speed. The board lists the speeds it supports, and it can list a lower maximum when every slot is filled. A speed the board does not support is not the speed you will get. The kit may also start at a lower standard speed until you turn on the memory profile in the firmware. The sheet names that profile. If you will not turn it on, price the speed you will actually run.

A kit of two sticks is sold and tested as a pair. Two single sticks of the same name, bought on their own, are a different listing. They can work, and they can fail to run at the kit speed. One stick of a larger capacity is a different product from two sticks that add up to the same capacity, and it leaves a channel empty until you add a second stick. Read the board's maximum capacity and the maximum per slot, and fill the slots the board sheet says to fill first. A kit that exceeds the board is the wrong kit.

Processors, motherboards, and combos

The socket name on the processor sheet has to be the socket name on the motherboard sheet. A similar word is not a match. A board for one socket does not take a processor built for another. Inside one socket, a board can still refuse a newer processor until its firmware is updated. Read whether this board supports this processor before the first start, or only after an update. If the board will not start with this processor until that update is done, you need a way to apply it. The sheet says whether the board can be updated with no processor installed. If it does not say, pause.

Some processors include a cooler. Some do not. A missing cooler is another purchase, and the case has a limit on cooler height. A cooler that does not fit is a second problem. The sheet says what is in the box.

A processor and a board sold as one package is a bundle. Price the processor you would buy and the board you would buy, sold separately, in the same condition. Compare that sum with the combo's checkout. A combo can pair a processor you want with a board missing the memory generation, the M.2 slots, or the power connectors you need. Count only the board you would actually use. How to take the package apart, including a piece you would not buy, is in Black Friday bundles: real savings or padding? Power supplies

The wattage printed on the supply is the number to set next to what the graphics card and the rest of the computer ask for. A higher wattage is unused room. A lower wattage than the parts require is the wrong supply. The efficiency badge on the sheet is a separate line. A higher badge on that scale means less of the electricity from the wall becomes heat, at the loads the badge defines. It does not raise the wattage on the label.

Count the connectors the board, the card, and the drives need. A modular supply lets you leave unused cables out. Those cables have to be the ones built for this supply. A cable from another supply can seat in the socket and still be wired differently. Use this supply's cables. The case sheet names the size the case takes, such as ATX or the smaller SFX. A supply that does not fit is not a deal. Read the warranty years on this listing. A supply with no warranty left is a different offer from a new one that still has its years.

Open-box and refurbished parts

An open-box or refurbished graphics card, drive, memory kit, or supply can beat new, or lose once new is on sale. The comparison between conditions, including the warranty and the return window on each listing, is in refurbished vs new. On a part, also match the variant. Less memory on a card, a different capacity or interface on a drive, or a memory kit someone split into single sticks is a different product. An open-box card should include the power adapter the new listing includes.

A drive's remaining writes are not printed in one shared way. Read what this listing says was tested or replaced, and skip a part whose warranty the listing does not describe. New, open-box, and refurbished do not share a chart.

The number next to "was," "list," "compare at," or a percent off is a reference the seller chose. Checkout, and this variant's recent selling prices, are the facts. When to wait

Wait if the socket, the memory generation, the drive interface, the wattage, or a measurement is missing. Wait if a combo only looks cheap next to prices for pieces you would not buy. Wait if firmware support for this processor is unclear, or if the supply's cables and connectors do not match the card. Wait if checkout is lower only next to a reference price, or if the chart belongs to another memory amount, another capacity, or another condition. Compare Friday and Monday in Black Friday vs Cyber Monday, and an early label in early Black Friday deals. Buy when the cart matches the sheet and checkout is at or under this variant's recent low. A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the sheet, and this variant's prices.

  1. 1Is this the same graphics card I compared, with the same memory amount, memory type, and cooler, or a different board that shares the chip name?
  2. 2Does the card fit the case, and does the power supply have the wattage and the connectors the sheet asks for?
  3. 3For a drive, do the interface, the generation, and the stick length match a free slot, and which ports does that slot turn off?
  4. 4Does the sheet name DRAM or DRAM-less, TLC or QLC, the terabytes-written rating, and the warranty years?
  5. 5Am I dividing checkout by the labeled capacity, in the same unit, for a size I will use?
  6. 6Does the memory kit match the board's generation and supported speed, and is it a tested kit or two sticks sold alone?
  7. 7Do the processor and the board name the same socket, and what do those two pieces cost separately if the cart is a combo?
  8. 8Is checkout at or under this exact variant's recent low, in this condition, with the warranty this listing actually includes?

When those match, the deal is this part.

Questions people ask

Is a last-generation graphics card still a good deal?

A previous-generation card is a sound buy when it does the work you have and its checkout is at or under that card's own recent prices. A newer card does not make the older one faster. Another memory amount, another cooler, or another generation has its own chart.

Why does a drive show less space than the number on the box?

The box counts a terabyte as one trillion bytes. The system often counts a terabyte as a larger binary unit, so the same bytes look smaller. Formatting uses a little more. The gap is the counting method, not storage missing from the drive.

Will a faster memory kit run at the speed on the label?

Only when the motherboard supports that speed and you turn on the memory profile the sheet names. Until then the kit can run at a lower standard speed. A speed the board does not list is not the speed you will get.

How do I compare the price of two drives with different capacities?

Divide each checkout price by the labeled terabytes on that box, using the same unit for every drive. Compare those results for drives you would actually fill. A lower price per terabyte on a drive that will not fit the slot, or that you will not fill, is a price for a different purchase.

Is a processor and motherboard combo cheaper than buying both?

Price the processor and the board you would buy on their own, in the same condition, and compare that sum with the combo's checkout. A combo that pairs the right processor with a board missing the slots you need is a different purchase. How to take a package apart is in the bundles guide.

What should I check on an open-box graphics card or drive?

Match the exact variant, the included adapters, and the warranty on this condition. An open-box card and a new one do not share a chart. What refurbished and open-box terms change, versus new, is in the refurbished guide.
