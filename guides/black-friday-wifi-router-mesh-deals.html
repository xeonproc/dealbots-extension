Wi-Fi generation, and when an older one is enough

A box can say Wi-Fi 7, Wi-Fi 6E, Wi-Fi 6, Wi-Fi 5, or only "Wi-Fi." On a sheet those generations also appear as 802.11be, 802.11ax, 802.11ac, or 802.11n. Wi-Fi 6E is Wi-Fi 6 plus a 6 GHz band. Wi-Fi 7 is the next generation. Write the full name from the cart and match it to the sheet. "New" means the unit has not been owned. If the sheet names no generation, pause.

Read the Wi-Fi line on each phone, laptop, television, and console. A device connects at the newest generation both it and the router support, so a Wi-Fi 7 router leaves a Wi-Fi 5 phone on Wi-Fi 5. An older generation is enough when your devices top out there, the rooms are covered, and updates are still coming.

Dual-band, tri-band, and what the label leaves out

Dual-band means two radios, almost always 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. Tri-band means three, and the sheet has to name them. It can be 2.4 GHz plus two 5 GHz radios, with no 6 GHz band. It can be 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz. Both are sold as tri-band. Only the second is the extra band on Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7. A quad-band line means four radios. The sheet names them.

The 2.4 GHz band reaches farther through walls and is crowded with older devices. The 5 GHz band is quicker nearby. The 6 GHz band is less crowded and fades sooner through walls, so it helps close by more than at the far end of the house. Wi-Fi 5 and Wi-Fi 6 devices leave a 6 GHz radio idle. Same generation with a different band list is a different product, and it has its own price.

Why the speed on the box is a total

The large number on the box is a sum. A class name such as AX3000 or BE11000 puts the generation in the letters and a total in the number. AX points at Wi-Fi 6, AXE at Wi-Fi 6E, and BE at Wi-Fi 7. The number adds each radio's theoretical maximum. The sheet lists the split. A phone or a laptop uses the radio it is connected to. It does not collect every radio's maximum.

"Up to" is a ceiling: a wide channel, a short distance, and little interference. Usable speed sits below the link rate a device shows. Wi-Fi 7 can use more than one band at once only when the router and the device both support that.

One router or a mesh, and 2-pack versus 3-pack

One router fits when a single well-placed spot already covers the rooms you use. A mesh system is two or more nodes under one network name. It fits when another floor, a far room, or a thick wall leaves a dead spot you use. The coverage figure on the carton is the maker's estimate for an open space, and walls shrink it. Place a node where it still hears another node, partway toward the weak room, not in the dead spot.

A 2-pack and a 3-pack are different products. Price the nodes you will place against a 2-pack plus one matching node later. A later node has to be the same family and generation, or it will not join. In some packs only one unit faces the modem, and a satellite cannot replace it. A range extender repeats a signal and spends airtime to do it. It is a node of this system only when the sheet says so. Wired backhaul and port speeds

Backhaul is the link between nodes. A wireless link shares the air with your devices. Dual-band uses the same radios as your phones. Some tri-band systems reserve one radio for the link between nodes. A 6 GHz radio may instead serve devices that can use it. Read which job the sheet describes. Ethernet between the nodes lets the wireless link sit idle.

A gigabit port is rated at 1 gigabit per second. Multi-gig ports are commonly rated at 2.5, 5, or 10. A 100 megabit port caps whatever is plugged into it, and "gigabit Wi-Fi" on the box can still hide a slower LAN port. Multi-gig matters on the WAN port when the plan is faster than 1 gigabit per second, and on a LAN port when two wired devices transfer faster than that. On a slower plan, gigabit already covers downloads. The slower end of the cable wins. The cable is often not in the box, and a node with no Ethernet port cannot take a wired link.

Your internet plan is the cap

The router does not raise the speed the internet provider sells. Write down the plan's download and upload rates. A computer plugged into the modem or gateway, with Wi-Fi off on that computer, shows what the line is delivering. Traffic to the internet is limited by the slowest of the plan, the Ethernet port, and the Wi-Fi link.

A newer generation is worth it when your devices can use it, when many are on at once, or when the router you have is slower than the plan. Wi-Fi 6 and later were designed to share a crowded network among more devices than Wi-Fi 5. A backup to a drive in the house can still exceed the plan. Pay for that only if you will use it.

The provider's modem or gateway

The provider's box may be a modem, or a gateway that combines a modem, a router, and Wi-Fi. A router you buy expects Ethernet from that box. A mesh pack with no port for a cable or fiber line is the Wi-Fi side. It does not replace the modem. If the plan requires the provider's equipment, a discount on your own modem does not apply.

Your router behind a gateway that still routes is two routers, and two layers of address translation. Some games, calls, and devices that need a direct path behave worse that way. Bridge mode, modem-only mode, or IP passthrough, whichever name this gateway uses, hands routing to your router. Read what else the box does, including a phone line, before you change it. If there is no such setting, find out whether your own router is allowed before you buy. A rental fee lasts until you return that equipment. A modem you buy has to be allowed for the plan and rated for its speed.

Features that need a subscription

Security tools, parental controls, and finer device priority can sit behind a subscription. The sale price is the box. Add the monthly or yearly fee for as long as you will keep those features. A trial that renews costs money unless you cancel on the printed terms.

Add the fee only for features you will use. Controls on the phone or laptop can overlap the router plan. Leave a security suite out when firmware patches stay free, and include the plan when the sheet ties patches to it. A protection plan in the cart is a separate purchase, priced in extended warranties and protection plans.

Firmware updates and old clearance models

A router sits on the path of every device, so firmware that fixes security problems is part of the purchase. Read how long this model receives updates, and the date of the latest firmware if the maker publishes one. If the support page no longer lists the model, further updates are unknown.

A very low price several generations back can be stock the maker no longer patches. That is a false bargain: unfixed problems stay on the path of every device. An older generation that still receives updates, and that matches your devices and your plan, can be the better buy. WPA3 is the newer security mode. A sheet that lists only an older mode is a smaller offer, and a device can refuse a mode it no longer accepts.

When to wait

Wait if the generation, the bands, the pack size, the port speeds, or the sheet is missing. Wait if a subscription you will not keep is in the total, if firmware updates are unclear, or if the gateway cannot be set so that one router is in charge. Wait if checkout is lower only next to a reference price. Compare Friday and Monday in Black Friday vs Cyber Monday, and an early label in early Black Friday deals. Buy when the cart matches the sheet and checkout is at or under this pack's recent low. A delivery date is in holiday shipping cutoffs.

A short checklist

Answer these from the cart, the sheet, and this pack's prices.

  1. 1Which Wi-Fi generation is in the cart, and which generation does each of my devices list?
  2. 2Which bands does the sheet list, and is "tri-band" a 6 GHz radio or a second 5 GHz radio?
  3. 3Is the speed on the box the sum of the radios, and which of those radios will my devices use?
  4. 4How many nodes will I place, and what does one matching node cost if I add it later?
  5. 5What speed are the WAN and LAN ports, and can I run Ethernet between the nodes?
  6. 6What are the plan's download and upload speeds, and is this router faster than the line I already have?
  7. 7Will the provider's gateway stay in routing mode, and what does a rental fee add over the time I would keep it?
  8. 8What do the subscription years I will keep cost, is this model still receiving firmware updates, and is checkout at or under this pack's recent low?

When those match, the deal is this router or this mesh pack.

Questions people ask

Is a Wi-Fi 6 or Wi-Fi 5 router still a good deal?

An older router is a sound buy when your devices top out at that generation, updates are still coming, and the ports keep up with the plan. A newer router does not raise a device past the generation it supports.

Why is one device so much slower than the number on the box?

The big number adds each radio's theoretical maximum, and one device uses only the radio it joined. Up to is a ceiling in ideal conditions, and the internet plan can sit lower still.

Should I buy a single router, a 2-pack, or a 3-pack?

Price the nodes you will actually place. Compare a 3-pack with a 2-pack plus one matching node later. A node from another line will not join.

When does a multi-gig Ethernet port matter?

It matters when the plan, or a transfer between wired devices in the house, is faster than 1 gigabit per second. On a slower plan, a gigabit port already covers internet downloads.

How do I count a security or parental-control subscription?

Add the fee for every period you will keep. A renewing trial costs money unless you cancel on the printed terms. Features you will not use stay out of the total.

How do I tell a real low from an old router that is no longer updated?

Check that this model still receives firmware updates, then read this pack's own recent prices. Another pack size or another generation has its own chart.
