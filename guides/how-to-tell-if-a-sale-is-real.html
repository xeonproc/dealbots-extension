How to tell if a sale is real

Updated October 17, 2026

A sale badge is a claim. The price you pay is the fact. A crossed-out "was" price can sit on an ordinary selling price, and the percent beside it is only as honest as that reference.

Work through the checks below before you treat a percent-off badge as a real sale.

Why the "was" price is unreliable

The number next to "was," "list," or "compare at" is not a receipt. The page does not have to show that anyone paid it, that it lasted, or that it matched this count, size, color, and condition. The seller sets that reference. Raise it, leave the selling price alone, and a badge can claim a large percent off while checkout stays the same. That is a fake discount: arithmetic on a number the seller chose, not evidence the price is low.

Trust two comparisons more than the badge. If the "was" price is far above other current offers for the same item and the same count, the reference is inflated. If the dollar price matches what the item has already been selling for, the percent off is a costume.

The markup-then-discount trick

The usual trick is short. A seller lifts the list price for a few days, sometimes only on that product page, then discounts it back to the old everyday price. The cart total does not move. A slower version leans on a suggested price the item almost never sells at, so ordinary weeks can all be labeled a sale.

Check that the comparison is the same product. A bundle, a smaller count, or a refurbished unit can borrow a percent off the full-size list price. The badge math can be correct and still describe a different item. Ask what checkout will charge after any code, before a mail-in rebate you might not get, and whether this dollar amount has already shown up with no sale label. If it has, you are looking at the regular price.

Compare the price with the recent low

A price history checker replaces the "was" price with prices people actually paid. For most products, look at the low over 60 to 90 days. Sixty days is enough to see past a brief markup. Ninety days is better when the item already goes on sale every month or two, because a short chart makes a routine low look special.

Match the variant. A price at or under that 60 to 90 day low, for the same count, size, color, and condition, is a real candidate. A price that sits on the ordinary selling price is the normal price with a new label. A dip that only beats yesterday, after yesterday jumped, is the markup trick on a chart. An open-box low, or a low for a smaller count, is a different product.

No chart? Search the product name plus "price" and read deal-community posts from the last two or three months. Keep the dollar amounts, and ignore percentages copied from the product page. Check the price per unit

A lower package price can be a worse deal. Divide what you will pay by the count, weight, loads, or months of use, and compare that with a smaller pack of the same product. Skip the multipack's own "was" price.

The sale pack can cost less overall and more per unit. The comparison unit can be a different formula, a travel size, or a refill that assumes you already own the hardware. A subscription can look cheap on the first order and step up after that. Price the orders you will keep. If the unit price is worse, the percent off does not repair it. "Is this a good price?" means the item you will use up.

Coupon codes and the fine print

A code can be the whole discount, or a price checkout will not honor. Before you count it, look for a minimum that forces extra items into the cart, an "up to" percent that excludes this item, and limits such as one per customer, new customers only, or the first subscription order. A mail-in rebate is not part of the price until it is approved. Shipping, a required membership, or a mandatory add-on can cost more than the cut. A timer tells you when the label expires, not whether the price is low.

Apply the code, let the cart recalculate, and judge that total. If the code fails, the deal you were reading is gone.

Seasonal timing, including Black Friday

Black Friday, end-of-season clearance, back-to-school, and holiday weekends change headlines more reliably than they change lows. Some prices really drop. Others repeat a low from the last month or two. Keep the 60 to 90 day test. A doorbuster that matches last month's low is last month's price with a seasonal name.

Prices sometimes dip the week before a named event and rebound on the day, so waiting is a bet once you already have the recent low. Clearance on seasonal goods, such as outerwear, holiday decor, and outdoor gear, often improves as the season ends, and a popular size can sell out. A claim that prices rise after the weekend is another "was" price, pointed forward.

Is this a good price? A short checklist

Answer these from checkout and from recent prices, not from the badge.

  1. 1What will I pay after codes, shipping, and any required add-on, before an unapproved rebate?
  2. 2Did people pay the "was" price, or did the seller only print it?
  3. 3Is today at or below the 60 to 90 day low for this same item?
  4. 4Is the price per unit better than a smaller size?
  5. 5Does the coupon exclude this item, demand a minimum, or apply only once?
  6. 6If the event is seasonal, is the price under the recent low?

When the checkout price is at or under that low, the unit price is not worse, and the fine print leaves the savings in place, the sale is real. If the recent low is unclear, skip the offer.

Questions people ask

How do I tell if a sale is real?

Compare the checkout price, not the was price, with what the same item sold for over the last 60 to 90 days. A real sale is at or under that recent low, and the coupon or shipping terms do not take the difference back.

What is a fake discount?

A fake discount is a sale label built on a reference price shoppers were not actually paying. The usual version raises the list price, then discounts it back to the old everyday price. The percentage looks large and the amount due does not change.

How do I know if this is a good price?

A good price is the checkout total for the exact item you will buy: at or under its recent low, with a per-unit cost that is not worse than a smaller size, and with no code, shipping rule, or rebate catch that puts the savings back.

How far back should a price history checker look?

Use 60 to 90 days for most products. Sixty days gets past a short markup. Ninety days is the safer window when the item already goes on sale every month or two. Match the count, size, color, and condition, or the low belongs to a different product.

Are Black Friday prices always lower?

Sometimes they are lower, and sometimes they repeat a low the item already hit. Treat the event as a reason to look, then compare that day's checkout price with the 60 to 90 day low. A seasonal headline is not itself evidence.
